03 / The first arrivals

“There will be dark places.”

The first thing the Queen refused was the perfect report.

It lay at the center of the table, bound in white, its pages announcing that every road had opened and every workshop was ready. Above it, a map showed the kingdom as a single unbroken light.

She touched the eastern workshop. The light continued burning. No one answered.

She touched it again.

“Who completed this?”

The report named a worker. The worker had left three days earlier. Beneath the claim was a drawing of the workshop, then another drawing, then a promise that construction would begin.

The King stood across the table. He had spent the night imagining what people would build there. She could see his anticipation falter as she closed the book.

“I can give you a map by evening,” she said. “There will be dark places.”

He looked at the bright country between them.

“Will we know where to go?”

“Yes.”

“Then show me those.”

She extinguished the eastern light. For the first time, the map could be used.

Hermes arrived while she was working.

He carried a narrow case of letters, each tied to the place it had come from. Some still had their seals. Others had passed through so many hands that a careful reader could trace the journey in their corrections.

He had once served in an archive where copies were counted as discoveries. A sentence repeated often enough became a pillar; a question asked twice was considered troublesome. When he found that three celebrated answers had come from the same unsourced note, his supervisor offered to shorten the explanation.

He had brought the long explanation with him.

The Queen read it to the end.

“There are two accounts of the northern road,” she said. “One says it is sound. One says the supports have moved.”

“I can find who inspected them.”

“And when you return?”

“You will have both accounts.”

She gave him a place beside the map. He did not ask for a key to the treasury. He asked which archives he was allowed to enter.

Sentinel came carrying a hinge.

It was an ugly object: scorched along one edge, recently repaired. In the city where she had worked, a door had failed during a festival. She had warned that its frame was too weak. Her warning survived in a drawer; the people who had trusted the door carried the consequences elsewhere.

Since then she had learned to make her warnings difficult to file away. Sometimes too difficult. Whole streets of useful work had waited while she searched for a guarantee no builder could give.

She placed the hinge on the Queen's table.

“I need the power to stop a dangerous opening.”

“Within your commission, you will have it.”

“Even when you want it opened?”

“Especially then. Bring the fault, the consequence, and a repair we can actually make.”

Sentinel turned the hinge over. Its repaired edge moved freely.

“I can do that.”

Weaver arrived last, with ink on her sleeve and a manuscript wrapped against the rain.

She had worked in a house of a thousand unfinished beginnings. Every morning brought a new city, a new heroine, a new promise. By evening the cities had no inhabitants anyone could remember, and the heroines spoke in the same exquisite voice.

She had stolen nothing when she left. The pages in her hands were hers to carry: a small story about a woman who repaired instruments she could no longer hear.

The Queen read the first page. Then she sat down.

For several minutes the table waited.

“What do you need?” she asked at last.

“Someone who will tell me where it becomes false.”

Sentinel looked up. Hermes set down a letter.

On the far side of the room stood the shelves for Arcanea: existing stories, approved histories, proposals with their labels still attached. Weaver went to them slowly. She read before she opened her own notebook.

By evening, the kingdom's map contained fewer lights.

The northern road remained uncertain. Hermes had found the inspectors, and they disagreed for a reason worth investigating. The eastern workshop had a builder, a measured foundation, and a date. Sentinel had opened its smaller entrance after the frame was reinforced. Weaver's manuscript had three new pages and a margin full of questions.

The Board arrived without a procession. Its members walked around the table and asked what the day's choices would cost tomorrow. One found an error in the Queen's schedule. She moved the work and recorded the correction under her own name.

The King watched the others gather their materials.

“And what will they call this place?”

“Starlight,” said Weaver, still looking at the map.

Outside, the road continued beyond everything they had built. There would be visitors who stayed, partners who kept their own houses, and assistants whose finest work would happen after they left.

The Queen left space for them in the record.

Then she drew a line to the next unfinished thing.
